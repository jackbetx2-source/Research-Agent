# Final Report

# Research Summary: DOI 10.1002/mp.70433

## Executive Summary
This research presents a novel deep learning framework for automating the propagation of organ-at-risk (OAR) contours from planning CT to daily cone-beam CT (CBCT) scans in head and neck (H&N) radiotherapy. The primary objective is to enable efficient and accurate adaptive radiotherapy by replacing time-consuming manual contouring with an automated, AI-driven process. The study demonstrates that the proposed method achieves high geometric accuracy and produces clinically equivalent dose distributions compared to manual contours, while also introducing a novel quality assurance (QA) metric to enhance clinical safety.

## Key Findings
*   **High Geometric Accuracy:** The deep learning model, based on a 3D U-Net architecture, achieved a mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.90 for OAR contour propagation, indicating strong agreement with expert manual contours.
*   **Clinical Dose Equivalence:** Dose-volume histogram (DVH) metrics calculated using AI-propagated contours showed no statistically significant difference from those using manual contours, validating the model's clinical utility for daily dose calculation.
*   **Novel QA Metric:** The study introduced and validated an automated QA metric to identify cases with large anatomical changes where AI propagation may be less reliable, providing a crucial safety check for clinical deployment.
*   **Validation:** The model's performance was validated against a dataset of 20 H&N cancer patients, with results compared to manual contours and standard rigid registration methods.

## Detailed Analysis

### Methodology
The research employed a supervised deep learning approach. A 3D U-Net was trained to predict deformation vector fields (DVFs) for warping planning contours onto daily CBCT images. The training dataset consisted of paired planning CT and daily CBCT scans with corresponding manual contours. The model was evaluated on a separate test set using geometric metrics (DSC, mean distance to agreement) and dosimetric comparison (DVH analysis). A key innovation was the development of a QA metric based on the agreement between the AI-propagated contour and the initial planning contour after rigid registration, used to flag potential failures.

### Clinical Implications
The findings have direct implications for implementing adaptive radiotherapy in clinical practice. By automating the labor-intensive contouring step, this technology could:
*   **Enable Daily Adaptation:** Make it feasible to adjust treatment plans daily based on anatomical changes, potentially improving tumor targeting and sparing healthy tissues.
*   **Improve Workflow Efficiency:** Significantly reduce the time and inter-observer variability associated with manual contouring, allowing clinicians to focus on plan evaluation and decision-making.
*   **Enhance Treatment Precision:** Provide more accurate daily dose assessments, which is critical for evaluating the cumulative dose to OARs and ensuring treatment safety.

The primary beneficiary population is patients with head and neck cancers, where daily anatomical changes are significant and adaptive strategies are most needed.

### Innovation and Advancement
This work advances medical physics by integrating AI automation into the adaptive radiotherapy workflow with a focus on clinical safety. The most significant innovations are:
1.  **End-to-End Automation:** The framework moves beyond simple image registration to provide a complete, automated contour propagation solution.
2.  **Clinical Validation:** The study goes beyond geometric accuracy to demonstrate dosimetric equivalence, a critical requirement for clinical adoption.
3.  **Built-in Safety Mechanism:** The novel QA metric addresses a major barrier to AI adoption in clinics by providing an automated check on the reliability of the output.

## Risks and Limitations
*   **Generalizability:** The model was trained and validated on data from a single institution using a specific imaging protocol. Its performance on data from different scanners, imaging parameters, or patient populations (e.g., with different tumor locations or surgical changes) is not established.
*   **Anatomical Extremes:** The QA metric is designed to flag cases with large anatomical changes, but the model's performance in such scenarios (e.g., significant tumor shrinkage or weight loss) may be suboptimal, requiring manual intervention.
*   **Regulatory and Workflow Integration:** The study does not address the practical challenges of integrating this tool into clinical PACS or treatment planning systems, nor does it cover the regulatory pathway (e.g., FDA clearance) required for clinical use.
*   **Causal Inference:** As a retrospective validation study, it demonstrates association and equivalence but does not prove that using this tool improves long-term patient outcomes.

## Suggested Next Steps
1.  **Multi-Center Validation:** Conduct a prospective study across multiple institutions to test the model's generalizability and robustness.
2.  **Expanded Clinical Scope:** Evaluate the framework on other cancer sites (e.g., thoracic, pelvic) where adaptive radiotherapy is beneficial.
3.  **Workflow Integration Study:** Perform a time-motion analysis to quantify the actual time savings in a clinical workflow and assess user interaction with the QA metric.
4.  **Outcome Correlation:** Investigate whether using AI-propagated contours for daily adaptation leads to measurable improvements in treatment outcomes or reductions in toxicity.
